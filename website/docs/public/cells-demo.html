A mini demo

We have created a minimal set of cell type definitions for three hippocampal classes, CA1, CA2 and CA3, shown below.

In the image below there are five cells. Cells 1, 2 and 3 were generated from CA1, CA2 and CA3 at one fifth of these counts: Cell 1 has 93 spots where the CA1 definition specifies 466. Inefficiency is the setting for that shortfall.

Cell 4 is a second CA2 cell, dimmer again by a factor of ten: 11 spots where CA2 specifies 120 once the Inefficiency is applied. Theta is the setting for what the Inefficiency leaves unexplained.

Cell 5 is a CA1 cell with one gene out of line, 14 Nptx1 spots where CA1 specifies 2 after the Inefficiency. Gamma is the setting for what the other two leave unexplained, gene by gene.

There is a fourth scaling factor, eta. Why is it not here?

It is held at 1 for simplicity. Eta is per gene across all cells, so it sits between the Inefficiency and theta in granularity. Once theta and gamma make sense, eta follows the same pattern.

Inefficiency

With every theta at 1, drag the Inefficiency slider. The cells were built at one fifth of the reference, so all three are called correctly at around 0.2. Move away from that and they start to break:

  • below about 0.15, Cell 1 is called CA3
  • above about 0.35, Cell 3 is called CA1
  • Cell 2 is never miscalled, because it carries Pcp4, which the other two classes barely express

In each cell's panel, next to the spot count, expected is the total the cell's true class specifies at the current settings. It turns orange when it departs from the observed count.

Theta

  • Indexed by cell and class. Cell 1's slider multiplies the CA1 definitions by theta, on top of the Inefficiency, while CA2 and CA3 stay scaled by the Inefficiency alone. The cell's counts are then scored against all three, and the probabilities follow.
  • Drag it away from that and the class specifies too many counts, or too few. An untouched rival eventually fits better and takes the call: Cell 1 goes to CA3 below 0.55 and again above about 2.
  • Cells 1, 2 and 3 need no scaling beyond the Inefficiency, so their theta stays at 1.
  • Cell 4 was built from the CA2 definitions but deliberately with far fewer spots, 11 where CA2 specifies 120 after the Inefficiency. At theta 1 it is miscalled as CA1. Pull its theta down to about 0.1 and CA2 fits.
For Cell 1, dragging the slider right and raising theta pushes the probability of CA3 to 100%. Why?

Theta multiplies the CA1 definitions, so the counts CA1 specifies grow well beyond what the cell actually has. CA2 and CA3 are untouched. CA1 becomes the least plausible of the three, and CA3, already the closer of the other two, takes the call.

A probability of 100% does not necessarily mean a perfect match. It can be the least bad of three poor options.

Cell 4 is called CA2 for any theta below about 0.96 and CA1 above it. The slider gives no reason to prefer one value over another. How does pciSeq choose theta rather than leaving it open?

It does not choose, it computes one theta for every candidate class:

theta(cell, class) = (N + rTheta - 1) / (rTheta + expected)

N is the cell's observed count and expected is what that class specifies after the Inefficiency. rTheta is a hyperparameter, 25 by default. For Cell 4, with N = 11:

CA1  expected  93  →  theta 0.30
CA2  expected 120  →  theta 0.24
CA3  expected 141  →  theta 0.21

Strictly, theta is not the raw ratio of observed to expected; rTheta shrinks that ratio toward 1. At low values the shrinkage is negligible and theta is data-driven; at high values theta remains close to 1 regardless of the counts. rTheta therefore controls how far the data are permitted to move theta. For Cell 4 the raw ratio is 11 / 120 = 0.09, which rTheta lifts to 0.24.

So we have three thetas for the cell, one per class. What happens next?

Each class is scored with its own theta, giving an adjusted set of definitions per class:

adjusted_expression(gene, class) = single_cell_data(gene, class) × Inefficiency × theta(cell, class)

The cell's gene counts are then scored against the adjusted expression above, assuming a negative binomial distribution, giving one log-likelihood per class. A softmax over those three totals gives the probabilities.

Gamma

  • Indexed by cell, class and gene, the finest of the three. Cell 5's slider scales the expected Nptx1 count for CA1 in that cell, and nothing else: not Nptx1 in other cells, not other genes in this one.
  • The observed counts never change. Gamma moves what the class expects, then every gene including Nptx1 is scored against the negative binomial and the contributions are summed.
  • Cell 5 has 14 Nptx1 spots where CA1 expects 2. At gamma 1 that excess makes CA3 the better fit and the cell is called CA3. Raise gamma to about 6.5, the ratio of observed to expected, and CA1 wins.
  • Theta cannot do this. It scales every gene together, so it cannot absorb one gene without distorting the other thirteen.
